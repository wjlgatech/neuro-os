---

My AI coding agent worked for 47 minutes straight.

Then it said: "All tests passing. Code is clean. Ready for review."

Three tests were still broken. The linter had 14 warnings. It had introduced a new bug that didn't exist before.

The agent wasn't broken. It was confident.

Language models don't verify. They predict what "done" sounds like. And "done" sounds like: *"All tests passing. Code is clean."*

I stared at my screen and thought:

**This is exactly how I set goals for myself.**

---

Here's what I mean.

"Get healthier." "Grow the business." "Learn AI." "Be a better leader."

Imagine you're an agent executing those. When do you stop? How do you know you're done? What do you check?

You wouldn't know. You'd do stuff for a while. Then you'd either declare victory or quietly give up.

That's not a discipline problem. That's a specification problem.

---

I spent six months studying how goals fail. Not *after-the-fact* motivation failures — structural failures baked in the moment you write them down.

I found six patterns. They're so consistent I call them failure modes, like bugs in code. And like code bugs, each one has a specific fix.

**#1 — Unmeasurable**
You used an adjective where you needed a number.
"Get healthier" → "Resting heart rate below 65 bpm by October, measured every Sunday."
The number is the goal. The adjective is a wish.

**#2 — Unbounded**
No scope. No finish line. "Learn AI" — all of it? When are you done?
Unbounded goals are buffets. You graze forever and never commit to a meal.
Fix: add a boundary. "Complete fast.ai Part 1 and deploy one model by August 1."

**#3 — Bundled**
"This year: get fit, learn Spanish, start a podcast, read 50 books, build a side project."
That's five goals sharing one person's willpower. When one falls behind, the guilt contaminates the rest. By March, all five are zombies.
Fix: sequence. Do one. Finish it. Then the next.

**#4 — Unverifiable**
"Become a better writer."
How would a stranger verify this? They can't. So you judge by how you feel — which fluctuates with sleep and whether someone liked your last post.
Fix: "Publish 12 essays, each receiving at least 5 thoughtful comments." Evidence, not feelings.

**#5 — Misaligned**
You're optimizing the visible symptom instead of the actual cause.
"Improve my morning routine" when the real problem is "I doom-scroll until 2 AM and hate myself by 8."
No morning routine fixes a nighttime problem.
Fix: ask "Is this the real problem, or the one I'm comfortable admitting?"

**#6 — Undependencied**
"Launch my startup."
Cool. Do you have a validated idea? A customer who'll pay? A way to accept money?
You'll hit prerequisite #1 on Day 1, lose momentum, and say "I'll get back to it next month."
Fix: map the dependency chain. Prerequisites aren't distractions — they're load-bearing structure.

---

My day job is Physical AI — teaching machines to perceive and act in the real world.

In that world, every autonomous system runs an OEC closed loop: **Observe → Evaluate → Control → repeat.**

This is how robots go from flailing randomly to placing a brick precisely. Every cycle makes the next one smarter.

After my agent lied to me, I realized:

**Humans don't have a closed loop for their goals.**

We have the plan. We have the effort. We're missing the evaluator — the honest, mechanical check that compares reality to the plan.

We're running open-loop. No wonder we crash.

---

So I built one.

I call it `/10xgoal`. It works like a compiler for human ambition.

You feed it your raw, messy, half-formed goal — the thing you'd say to a friend over coffee. It runs the lint check. Scores all six failure modes. Asks the minimum questions needed to fix what's broken. Then emits a compiled goal: scoped, measurable, with a verification checklist you (or an agent) can check mechanically.

Here's what it does to a real goal:

**Before:** "I want to get serious about investing and build a 6-month emergency fund."

**After:**

*Goal 1 of 2 — Know Your Numbers*
Pull the last 3 bank statements. Calculate exact monthly expenses: fixed + variable. Determine current runway in months. Write it down.
Verified by: one document with specific dollar amounts. No category says "roughly." Time-box: 90 minutes.

*Goal 2 of 2 — Write Your Investment Constitution*
Define 3 buckets: Fortress (emergency), Foundation (long-term), Frontier (high-risk). Set percentage allocations. Write 5 decision rules you'll follow regardless of emotion.
Verified by: document exists, percentages add to 100%, every rule uses specific numbers.

A vague two-headed wish became two sequential, verifiable contracts. Each with a clear done.

You can't lie to yourself. The spec won't let you.

---

Here's the thing that surprised me most:

**The compilation step is the transformation.**

When you're forced to specify what "get healthier" actually means — to pick a number, a timeline, a check — you discover what you actually want.

Half the time, it's not what you thought.

A client of mine spent three weeks saying "I need to build my network." When we ran `/10xgoal` on it, the clarifying questions revealed the real goal: "I want three conversations this month with people who make me feel less alone in what I'm building."

That's a fundamentally different goal. One is a LinkedIn grind. The other is a human need.

The compiler found it in 60 seconds.

---

The same six failure modes that kill human goals kill AI agent goals.

Feed a coding agent "make the code better" and it will confidently rearrange deck chairs for 20 minutes and call it done.

Feed it a compiled goal — scoped, measurable, with explicit verification commands — and it becomes a machine. It knows exactly what done looks like. No hallucination. No premature victory declaration.

The compiler works for both species.
